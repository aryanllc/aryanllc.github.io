1. What is AI?

Artificial intelligence is a broad field of computer science concerned with building systems that can perform tasks associated with abilities such as recognizing patterns, understanding language, planning, predicting outcomes, selecting actions, or generating content.

A useful mental model

Think of AI as a collection of techniques for turning inputs into useful outputs. The input might be text, an image, audio, sensor data, or numbers. The output might be a classification, prediction, recommendation, decision, or generated result.

AI is not one machine and it does not always mean a humanoid robot. A spam filter, a speech recognizer, a recommendation system, an image classifier, a route-planning system, and a language model can all use AI while working in very different ways.

The word “intelligence” can make AI sound more human than it really is. Modern systems can be extremely capable at particular tasks while still failing in surprising situations. It is therefore useful to describe what a model actually does rather than assuming that it has human emotions, beliefs, or consciousness.

AI you may already use

AI can appear quietly inside ordinary apps. Search engines rank results. Maps estimate travel times. Email services filter spam. Cameras can detect scenes. Video platforms can recommend content. Accessibility tools can transcribe speech or describe images. Businesses can use models to forecast demand or identify unusual activity.

In many cases, the AI is one component of a larger product. The interface you see may be simple even though the system behind it uses statistics, machine learning, neural networks, databases, rules, or several of these together.

2. How does modern AI work?

There is no single recipe. A common machine-learning workflow, however, includes data, a model, training, evaluation, and inference.

Data

Data provides examples from which patterns can be learned. It may contain text, photographs, audio, measurements, transactions, or other records. Data quality, relevance, coverage, and labeling can strongly affect the resulting system.

Model

A model is a mathematical structure whose parameters can be adjusted. Different model families are suited to different tasks. Neural networks are one important family, but AI also includes search methods, probabilistic models, optimization, rules, and other approaches.

Training and inference

During training, parameters are adjusted to improve an objective using examples. During inference, the trained model is used on new inputs. Testing on unseen data is important because a model can perform well on familiar examples and poorly on new ones.

A simple example

Imagine a spam classifier. You give it many examples of messages labeled “spam” or “not spam.” The training process adjusts the model so its predictions become better according to the chosen objective. After training, a new email is passed through the model. The output might be a probability or classification that helps the application decide where to put the message.

This does not mean the model memorizes a perfect definition of spam. It learns patterns associated with the training data. If future messages are very different, the model can make mistakes. That is why evaluation, monitoring, and careful design matter.

3. Important AI concepts

These ideas are related, but they are not interchangeable.

Machine learning

Machine learning is a major part of AI in which systems learn patterns from data instead of relying only on hand-written rules. Supervised learning uses labeled examples; unsupervised methods can discover structure without the same labels; reinforcement learning learns through interaction and feedback.

Deep learning

Deep learning uses neural networks with multiple layers. These networks can learn representations that support tasks such as image recognition, speech processing, language modeling, and many generative applications.

Generative AI

Generative AI creates outputs such as text, images, audio, video, or code from learned patterns. A generated answer can be useful and fluent without being guaranteed to be correct, so verification remains important.

AGI

Artificial general intelligence is a broad, hypothetical idea about AI that could handle many different intellectual tasks with flexible competence. Researchers and technologists do not share one universal definition or one universally accepted test for AGI.

4. A short history of AI

AI has gone through periods of excitement, disappointment, new ideas, and renewed progress.

1940s–50s
Early work connected computation, logic, neural models, information theory, and questions about machine intelligence.
1956
The Dartmouth summer research project helped establish “artificial intelligence” as the name of a research field.
1970s–90s
Rule-based and expert systems became important, while changing expectations and funding led to several periods sometimes called AI winters.
2000s–2010s
Large datasets, specialized hardware, and improved deep-learning methods produced major gains in vision, speech, and language tasks.
2020s
Large generative models became widely available through consumer products, developer tools, and creative applications.

5. What AI can and cannot do

Being useful does not mean being infallible.

Strengths

  • Find patterns in large datasets.
  • Automate repetitive classification or prediction tasks.
  • Generate drafts and assist with coding, writing, or creative work.
  • Analyze complex inputs such as images, audio, and text.
  • Provide interactive tools for learning and accessibility.

Limitations

  • Models can generate inaccurate or unsupported information.
  • Training data can contain bias or unwanted correlations.
  • Performance can drop on situations unlike the training examples.
  • Privacy, security, copyright, and misuse can create real-world risks.
  • Human review is important when mistakes have serious consequences.

Why can an AI answer confidently and still be wrong?

Many generative systems are optimized to produce plausible sequences or outputs. Fluency is not the same as factual verification. A response may sound polished because the model has learned strong patterns in language while still producing a statement that is unsupported, outdated, or simply incorrect. For important questions, treat AI output as something to check rather than an automatic source of truth.

7. AI vocabulary

These terms show up often when learning about AI.

Algorithm

A procedure or set of steps used to solve a problem or transform inputs.

Dataset

A collection of examples or records used for training, evaluation, or analysis.

Parameter

A value inside a model that can be adjusted during training.

Token

A unit used by many language models to represent text, such as a word, part of a word, or punctuation.

Prompt

An instruction or request supplied to a generative AI system.

Hallucination

An informal term for generated information that sounds plausible but is inaccurate or unsupported.

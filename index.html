User Guide & Documentation Hub

The sections below give you a practical introduction to AI: what it means, how learning systems work, what modern generative models do, why they make mistakes, and how to think about AI responsibly.

What is artificial intelligence?

Artificial intelligence, or AI, is a broad area of computer science concerned with building systems that can perform tasks commonly associated with aspects of intelligence. Those tasks can include recognizing patterns, interpreting language, predicting outcomes, ranking choices, planning actions, identifying objects, or generating new content. AI is not one specific program or one physical machine. It is a collection of techniques that can be combined in many different ways.

The term can be confusing because different applications use very different approaches. A recommendation system might estimate which items are likely to interest a user. A vision system might classify objects in photographs. A language model might predict and generate sequences of text. A game-playing system might search through possible moves. All can belong to the broader AI field even though their internal designs are not the same.

AI in everyday software

You may already interact with AI without noticing it. Search engines use ranking systems to organize information. Maps can estimate travel times and suggest routes. Email software can identify unwanted messages. Cameras can detect scenes or objects. Streaming platforms can recommend content. Accessibility tools can transcribe speech, describe images, or assist with communication.

In these products, AI is usually one component of a larger system. The application may also contain databases, ordinary software rules, user-interface code, security controls, and many other parts. Calling a product “AI-powered” therefore tells you very little by itself; the useful question is what the system actually does and how it is evaluated.

Machine learning

Machine learning is one of the most important branches of AI. Instead of writing a separate rule for every situation, developers can train a model using examples. In supervised learning, examples are paired with labels such as “spam” or “not spam.” The model adjusts its internal parameters during training so its outputs better match the desired results.

Other approaches learn from unlabeled data or from interaction and feedback. Regardless of the method, the basic idea is that a model can use experience represented in data to make predictions or decisions on new inputs. Evaluation on data that was not used to train the model is essential because a model can memorize training examples without learning patterns that generalize well.

Deep learning and neural networks

Deep learning is a family of machine-learning methods built around neural networks with multiple layers. Each layer can transform information into a representation that is useful for later layers. During training, numerical parameters are adjusted so the network becomes better at an objective.

Neural networks can contain very large numbers of parameters, and modern systems often require substantial computing resources for training. They have been especially successful in areas such as computer vision, speech processing, language modeling, and generation. However, a neural network is still a mathematical model. Human-like language or visual behavior does not automatically establish consciousness, emotions, or human experience.

Generative AI

Generative AI refers to systems that produce new outputs based on patterns learned from data. Depending on the system, an output may be text, an image, audio, video, code, or another kind of content. Large language models generate text by estimating likely token sequences conditioned on their input and previous context.

Generative systems can be useful for brainstorming, tutoring, summarizing, drafting, prototyping, and many other tasks. Their outputs still need appropriate checking. A model can produce a fluent answer that is incomplete, outdated, incorrectly reasoned, or unsupported. The more important the decision, the more important independent verification becomes.

AGI and broad intelligence

Artificial general intelligence, often shortened to AGI, is a broad and hypothetical concept about systems that could handle many different intellectual tasks with flexible competence. There is no single universally accepted definition or test for AGI. For that reason, claims about whether a particular system is “AGI” can depend heavily on the definition being used.

It is useful to separate demonstrated capabilities from labels. A system can be excellent at one task while struggling with another. Thinking in terms of measurable abilities makes comparisons clearer than assuming that a broad label automatically describes every part of a system.

How an AI system learns a pattern

Imagine a program that needs to identify unwanted email. Developers can assemble a dataset containing many examples and labels. A model processes the examples and produces predictions. A training process compares the predictions against the desired outcomes and uses an optimization algorithm to update the model's parameters. The process is repeated many times. Once training is finished, the model can be evaluated on previously unseen examples. If performance remains strong on new data, that provides evidence that the model learned useful patterns rather than simply memorizing the training set.

Real systems are more complicated. Teams choose architectures, prepare datasets, evaluate results, monitor failures, test robustness, and decide how a model will be deployed. They may also add filters, retrieval systems, tools, human review, and other software around the model. The complete application is therefore more than the model itself.

More to understand

AI is powerful, but useful understanding includes its limitations, evaluation methods, and real-world risks.

Data quality

A model learns from the information available to it. Missing, noisy, unbalanced, or misleading data can affect results. Data collection and labeling can therefore be as important as model architecture.

Generalization

A good model should perform usefully on new inputs, not only examples seen during training. Testing on unseen data helps measure whether patterns generalize.

Hallucinations

“Hallucination” is an informal term often used when a generative AI system gives information that sounds plausible but is inaccurate or unsupported.

Bias and fairness

Models can reflect patterns and biases present in their training data or design choices. Responsible development includes measuring important failure modes and considering who may be affected by them.

Privacy

AI products can involve sensitive inputs or personal information. Good practice includes minimizing unnecessary collection, protecting data, and explaining important data uses.

Human review

When an error could have serious consequences, a human review process can add an important layer of checking. AI output should not automatically be treated as verified fact.

Quick timeline

1950s–60s
Early research explored machine reasoning, symbolic methods, neural models, and questions about machine intelligence.
1970s–90s
Rule-based and expert systems became important while funding and expectations fluctuated through several “AI winter” periods.
2000s–2010s
More data, better algorithms, and stronger computing hardware helped machine learning and deep learning become highly effective in many tasks.
2020s
Generative AI systems became widely available through consumer products, developer platforms, and creative tools.

Learning checklist

After reading the guide, you should be able to explain these ideas in your own words.

  • What AI means and why it is a broad field.
  • How machine learning differs from hand-written rules.
  • Why data quality and unseen-data testing matter.
  • What neural networks and deep learning are.
  • What generative AI produces.
  • Why AI can produce confident but incorrect information.
  • What AGI means as a broad, unsettled concept.
  • Why privacy, bias, security, and human review matter.

About the project

AI Explained is an independent interactive AI project created to provide hands-on utilities for learning about artificial intelligence. The goal is to make core concepts easier to explore by combining plain-language documentation with browser-based interactive features.

The project is designed for curious learners who want more than a glossary. Visitors can explore concepts, compare related ideas, test their understanding, and use simple interactive demonstrations to build intuition. The site is intentionally lightweight so that the core learning experience can work as a modern web page without requiring an account.

The information provided on this project is educational. AI changes quickly, and individual models, products, laws, policies, and technical practices can change over time.
